# BCC-500

## Overview

The **BCC-500** was an innovative timesharing computer built by the Berkeley Computer Corporation in the late 1960s.

## Background

Several members of UC Berkeley's Project Genie—including Butler Lampson, Chuck Thacker, L. Peter Deutsch, and Mel Pirtle—left the university to form Berkeley Computer Corporation, aiming to create an advanced timesharing system building on their earlier work with the Berkeley Timesharing System.

## Technical Architecture

The BCC-500 featured six independent microcoded processors with 24-bit word width and 18-bit addressing, all sharing access to common memory. Two processors with expanded capabilities ran user code, while the other four each handled different parts of the operating system through a combination of microcode and macrocode.

## Fate and Legacy

Despite starting with $4 million in venture funding, BCC went bankrupt around 1970-1971 when their funding from Data Processing Financial & General suddenly stopped. The prototype was sold to the University of Hawaii, and after two years of refurbishment, it was successfully deployed as an ARPANET host until 1980.

The BCC-500 became the hub of the ALOHAnet, the pioneering wireless packet-switched network that inspired Ethernet. After BCC's collapse, several key employees went on to form the core of Xerox PARC's computer research group in 1970, where they continued influential work in computer science.

## Historical Significance

- **ARPANET Host**: Served as a network node from 1973-1980
- **ALOHAnet Hub**: Central system for the groundbreaking wireless network
- **Innovation Pipeline**: Bridge between UC Berkeley's Project Genie and Xerox PARC's legendary research
- **Architectural Innovation**: Multi-processor design with microcoded architecture ahead of its time
